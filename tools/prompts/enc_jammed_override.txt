The ship's lower-deck cargo bay, the same room as the reference image (same layout: vast metal hall, floor rails, the huge sealed cargo door at the far end buried in a mound of mold-covered debris, the raised glass control booth on the right wall), revisited weeks later. Shot as a photograph: a frame from a 1970s science-fiction film in the idiom of Space: 1999 and Silent Running, 35mm Kodak film grain, a real practical set, deep focus. The lighting is much better now: the ceiling light panels have come back on in long rows, clean white work light washing the floor, so every detail reads clearly; the orange spore haze is thinner, lying low along the floor and pooling around the debris mound. It is still grimy: corroded plating, drifts of russet mold in the corners, old scorch marks.
Eye level, looking up the short metal stair to the control booth, the party's point of view. Inside the booth, lit by the glow of its console: a human skeleton in the rags of a crew jumpsuit still slumped in the operator's chair, one bony hand resting on the console. Everything the party needs is on that one console, in scale with the skeleton's hands: a red plastic key card, credit-card sized, pushed halfway into a slot near the skeleton's right hand; and at the console's left end, the door override, one small control among the others: a coaster-sized square flip-up cover hinged along its top edge and stuck part-open by a crust of russet mold, and under it a small round selector knob with two tiny lamps above it, the left amber and lit, the right dark green. Nothing mounted on the booth walls; no wall boxes, no pedestals, no levers or handles. Through the booth's glass, and far across the bay, a heavy sliding door in the south wall stands half-open mid-cycle, a column of orange spore haze rolling through the gap. Tempting, detailed, a puzzle waiting to be poked at. No people in the bay. No captions or watermark; any lettering on the console is too small to read at this distance.

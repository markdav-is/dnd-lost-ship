A curving corridor on the observation-deck ring of the ship, the moment the lights come back. Wide shot at eye level, looking down the length of the curving hall. The ceiling light panels have just come on in a rolling wave, bright and warm white, the ones nearest the camera fully on and the farthest few still flickering awake. The corridor is clean Space: 1999 production design underneath (cream wall panels, rounded doorframes, an orange stripe at waist height) but it has sat dark for a century: a fine film of dust on every surface, dust drifting in the new light like snow, a few cobwebs in the corners, faint russet mold stains low on the walls, scuffed footprints in the dust on the floor.
Coming down the corridor toward the camera, to the rescue: four aerialist androids from the ship's dance club. They are built exactly like the medical android in the second reference image: smooth, moulded, slightly glossy plastic skin, hairless sculpted heads, doll-like faces with calm painted eyes, visible seam lines at the neck and wrists. But these are party models, cast in bright candy colours instead of cream: one tangerine orange, one hot pink, one turquoise, one sunshine yellow. All four have a dancer's build: slight, slender, long-limbed, built for speed and grace. Two are clearly male and two are clearly female, and all four are joyful: big delighted grins, bright eyes, thrilled to be dancing again. They are still obviously moulded plastic dolls, but their sculpted looks borrow from mid-1970s television glamour. The male pair are sculpted as the classic rugged 70s TV action hero: square jaw, dimpled chin, a confident easy grin, and a sculpted plastic moulding of a sandy, side-parted, feathered 70s haircut. The female pair are sculpted as the iconic 70s pin-up poster girl: a huge dazzling toothy smile, and a sculpted plastic moulding of big, voluminous, feathered-back 70s flip hair. The plastic hair is the same candy colour as each body. Outfits vary slightly, all 1970s disco: the men in a sequinned open-collar jumpsuit and in flared trousers with a cropped glitter bolero; the women in a halter jumpsuit and in a spangled leotard with bell-bottoms. Two sprint along the floor in long, graceful, theatrical strides. The other two are mid-leap, not flying like Superman: each wears a chunky chrome jump belt with small gas jets at the hips, and they are caught at the top of a long bounding jump, upright, knees tucked like a dancer's grand jeté, twin jets of white gas puffing from their belts and kicking up the dust below. Behind them, far down the curve, the dance club door stands open. The coloured disco light and the mirror ball are INSIDE that room, seen through the doorway; the corridor itself is lit only by plain warm-white ceiling panels, with no coloured light spots on the corridor walls or floor.
Shot as a photograph: a frame from a 1970s British science-fiction television series in the idiom of Space: 1999, 35mm Kodak film grain, a real practical set with practical costumes, real light and dust. No people other than the four androids. No text, no signage, no lettering anywhere.

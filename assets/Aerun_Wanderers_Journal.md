# Chapter One: The World of Aerun

I live in a world of heat and coin. The sun hammers the life from anything that
stands still, the wind files the paint from the caravan boards, and a man's worth
is written in a ledger he will never be permitted to read.

There are no gods on Aerun. I have heard travelers ask for the temples and I have
watched the locals smile at them, the way you smile at a child who asks where the
ocean keeps its door. There is the House, and there is the ledger, and there is
the arena where the ledgers are settled. That is the whole of our theology.

This is my home, Aerun. It is a brutal place, but it is not a poor one. It is the
richest land under the sun, and that is precisely the trouble: everything here —
the water, the roads, the beasts, the people — belongs to somebody, and the
somebody is always a House.

## Overview of the Continent

Aerun lies across the world's waist like a bar across a door.

Understand the winds and you understand everything else. The westerlies come off
the open ocean heavy with rain, strike the **Ringing Mountains** that wall our
western coast, and give up every drop they carry on the seaward slopes. The far
side of those mountains gets nothing, and has gotten nothing for an age of the
world. That is the desert: not a punishment, not a curse — a rain shadow the size
of a continent. The green face of the Ringing Mountains looks out at the sea and
sings when the wind pours through its canyons. The inland face is bone.

Then there are the equatorial winds. They blow along our coasts without rest or
mercy, and they have drowned every fleet that ever tried to sail through them
from one hemisphere to the other. A captain of the northern seas does not sail
*around* Aerun. He sails *to* it. His cargo comes ashore at a portage city, rolls
across the continent on the **Ring Road**, and meets a southern ship on the far
coast. Aerun is not on the trade route. Aerun *is* the trade route, and the seven
cities collect their toll on every bolt of cloth, every ingot, every secret that
passes between the halves of the world.

By midmorning it is 100 degrees in the shade of a wagon. By late afternoon, 120,
and on the salt flats worse. Plan your day around it as we do: move at dawn, move
at dusk, and in the white hours lie still and spend as little of yourself as you
can.

## Metal

You will see less worked metal on Aerun than anywhere else you have traveled, and
it is not because we cannot get it. Tyr's mines pull iron from the mountains, and
the portage cranes swing crates of northern steel over the docks every day of the
year.

We simply have no use for the stuff, and neither will you.

Put on a steel breastplate at noon on the Ring Road and you have climbed into a
cook-pot. Metal drinks the sun. It sears bare skin, it gains weight with every
mile, and every pound of iron on your back is a pound of water you did not bring.
The caravan masters put it plainly: *better to carry water.* Aerunite arms and
armor are bone, chitin, horn, hardened leather, and good obsidian, and an
Aerunite smith who works metal at all works it at night. Steel here is a cargo,
not a tool. It passes through our hands on its way to cooler countries, and we
tax it, and we let it go, and we do not miss it.

## Psionics

Where other lands raise temples, Aerun raises **schools**.

Every soul on this continent carries a spark of the Will — a wild talent, we call
it, some small trick of mind the owner may barely notice: a knack for calming a
beast, a moment's warning before the dune shifts, a cup that slides to the hand.
Do not gamble against an Aerunite until you have watched him play.

Visitors are not exempt, only late. Stay on Aerun long enough — a season, two —
and the land gets into you; the talent surfaces like a splinter working its way
out. Those who have tasted the Spice come to it faster still.

The disciplined mind can make of that spark a fire. The psionic schools take
students from every House and every station — even the pits send their promising
young, on their masters' coin — and a graduated master is the most respected
figure in any city, consulted the way other lands consult their priests. When an
Aerunite says *the Will and the Way*, he is not being poetic. He is naming the
only power on this continent that no one is ashamed of.

## Magic

Now attend, because this will keep you alive: **magic is despised here**, and the
despising is not superstition.

Ask any child what made the Sea of Silt and they will tell you: wizards did. In
the elder days the defilers drew their power up out of the living land itself,
and where the green died the soil followed, until the heart of the continent
powdered into the gray dust-ocean you will smell on the wind off the heights long
before you see it. Whether every particular of the story is true I cannot say. The Silt Sea
is true enough.

So all magic is suspect — all of it. The honest hedge-wizard and the defiler look
identical to a gate clerk, and the gate clerk does not get paid to tell them
apart. When you arrive at any city they will ask you — *"What's your business?"* —
and while you answer, they will search your baggage. Sulfur, bat guano, crystal
foci, inscribed bone, anything on the contraband schedules: confiscated, sealed,
logged against your name, and — this being Aerun — **bonded, not stolen**. Declare
your exit port and your components will be waiting there in a sealed casket when
you leave. The Houses despise magic, but they would never insult property.

Cast openly in a city and no writ will save you. There are no jails to hold you
in. There are the pits, and there is the arena.

## A Short History, As We Tell It

Long ago — every telling disagrees on how long — the world was green and clever.
The old civilization of Vermoon built wonders we no longer have names for, and
towers of glass, and engines that thought.

Then the great dragons came, or arose, or were made — the tellings disagree here
too — and they broke that world. What their fires began, the dark time finished:
the wizards fed on the wounded land until the center of Aerun died, and the
rains forgot us, and the desert walked in and sat down and stayed.

What survived was not the strongest of us. It was the best organized. The
families who held the wells and moved the grain became the Houses, and the Houses
became Aerun.

We have been invaded — count the attempts on your fingers and borrow a friend's
hands. Crusades of foreign gods. Hosts of conquering races. Every one of them
broke against the same wall, and the wall was never made of soldiers. The
merchant kings do not defeat an invader; they *acquire* him. They buy his
captains, starve his supply, hire his enemies, and raise his grain prices, and in
a generation his grandchildren are clerking in a House emporium and arguing about
their pension terms. Faith is a fire and coin is the sand, and on Aerun the sand
always wins.

That is why there are no gods here. It is not that no one ever brought us any.
It is that none of them could meet their payroll.

# Chapter Two: Aerun Society

## The Dynastic Houses

Begin here, because everything else in this chapter is a footnote to this:
**seven Houses, seven cities, one continent.**

A House is a family the way the Ringing Mountains are a hill. Thousands of souls
share the name — the blood line at the center, the sworn lesser families around
them, and rings upon rings of agents, factors, guards, pilots, and laborers,
down to the pit slaves who wear the House glyph on their shoulder. The House
feeds you, waters you, schools you, employs you, marries you, judges you, and
buries you. Ask an Aerunite who he is and he will not name a race or a god. He
will name his House.

The **merchant kings** stand at the head of the blood lines — patriarchs and
matriarchs of the seven families, each ruling a city outright. No crowns; a crown
is an asset that cannot be sold. Their authority is the ledger, the water
schedule, and the Code, and it reaches every person inside their walls save the
transient traveler who has declared his business at the gate.

Beneath each great House swarm the **lesser families** — sworn lines like Ecaz,
Moritani, Richese, Vernius, and Fenring — bound by marriage, debt, and vassalage.
A lesser family manages an emporium, a waystation, a mine. A great one manages a
civilization.

The Houses maintain, as they have for centuries: **headquarters** in their home
city, a fortress of ledgers where the family lives; **emporiums** in every other
city, part warehouse, part embassy, part spy-house; **outposts** strung along the
Ring Road and the silt shelves; and shares in the great **caravans** — though the
caravans themselves, as I will explain, belong to no single House at all.

**Employment.** The Houses hire outsiders constantly — guards, scouts, hunters,
problem-solvers. House work pays well and feeds better, but read your terms
twice: a signed term of service places you under House law, and House law is the
Code, and ignorance of it has filled many a pit. An adventurer's rule of thumb:
take the work, refuse the tattoo.

**The Merchant Code** is real. Travelers laugh at this — a continent of
slavers and monopolists, governed by an honor code? Laugh quietly. The Code is
the one law that binds House to House: agreements written are kept; cargo bonded
is returned; a declared price is a price; arena verdicts are final; the
caravan and the waystation water are neutral ground. Houses that cheat each
other outside the Code invite the one thing no ledger survives — every other
House, at once, closing its roads to them. The Code is not morality. It is
infrastructure.

## The House Champions

Here is the question every visitor asks on his first day: *where are the
armies?* Seven rival powers, one road binding them, wealth beyond counting — and
no legions on the march, ever. The answer is the finest of Aerun's inventions.

**The Houses do not wage war. They wage champions.**

When Houses come to a dispute the ledgers cannot settle — a route, a water
right, a defaulted bond, an insult too public to price — they do not raise
armies, which are ruinously expensive and bad for trade. They post the stakes
under the Code and send their champions to the arena. Two go in. The stakes go
to the House still standing. I have watched a city quarter, four caravans, and a
year of a silver mine's yield change families in the time it takes to bleed.

A champion is no mere gladiator. Each House keeps a stable of them, raised from
the most promising blood of the family and the pits alike, trained from
childhood in the schools and in the sand. The bond between a champion and their
House is sealed in **Spice** — the House provisions its champions with the most
expensive substance on Aerun, and the champion's focused, Spice-fed Will is knit
to the family that feeds it. Ask why a champion never defects and you have
answered your own question: the House holds the supply. Old travelers will tell
you other lands manage this arrangement with priest-bureaucrats and holy
warrants. We find our way tidier. The blue-eyed sword loves the hand that feeds
it, and no god need be troubled.

The great champions are the closest things Aerun has to worship. Their bouts
empty the streets; their names outlive their kings — **Hamanu of Urik**, undefeated
across a span no one honestly remembers the start of; **the Oba of Gulg**, who
fights, they say, like the forest itself; **the Shadow King of Nibenay**, whom no
one has seen fight and whose opponents concede. And above the champions hangs
the old whisper, the one thing spoken of only at the fire's edge: a champion who
climbs too high, who takes too much Spice and too much power into the flesh,
begins the **dragon road**. The dragons that broke the old world were champions
once. The Houses swear the road is closed. The desert people say the desert
remembers otherwise.

And Tyr's champion seat is *empty*. Attend the next section.

## The Nobility

Within each House, the noble tier is the blood line and the sworn lesser
families: those born to the name rather than employed by it. They command the
emporiums, hold the water schedules, officer the champions' stables, and marry
as the ledger directs. A House noble is raised on two catechisms — the Code, and
the family's ancestor-rolls, recited the way other lands recite scripture. The
family is the religion; the wedding contract is its sacrament; the ledger is its
book of days.

## The Freemen

Below the blood and above the pits stand the freemen — artisans, farmers,
drovers, clerks, hunters, the great middle of every city. A freeman is
house-aligned but not house-owned: he wears the glyph, pays the tithe, drinks
the House water, and may leave — in principle. In practice his debts, his
family, and his water ration are all denominated in House scrip, and freedom
that cannot afford the road is a modest sort of freedom. Still, he is proud of
it, and he is right to be. The distance between the freeman and the pit is the
width of a signature, and every freeman knows someone who signed.

**Transients.** Then there is you. The traveler who declares at the gate —
*"What's your business?"* — stands outside the House system entirely: not owned,
not protected, logged. Your name, your business, your components-bond, and your
declared exit port follow you from city to city faster than you travel (the
Houses share the registry; it is the one list they never cheat on). Transient
status is genuine liberty and genuine exposure in equal measure. No House feeds
you, no House waters you, and if you die in a ditch the road crew that finds you
will check the registry, note your exit port, and charge the burial to your
bond.

## The Slaves

I will not soften this: Aerun runs on slaves, more than any land I have walked,
and an Aerunite will explain the arithmetic to you without a flicker of shame.

Magic might have watered the fields and raised the walls — magic is forbidden.
Metal engines might have done it — metal cooks its handlers and drinks water in
weight. What remains is muscle, and muscle is bought. Every race under the sun
sweats side by side in the fields, the salt pans, the portage cranes, and the
road gangs: humans, dwarves, elves, muls, derrow, half-giants, thri-kreen, and
every mingling of them, sorted by strength and skill and never by blood.

Know the trades of the pit: **gladiators**, the aristocracy of the owned, some a
bout or two from buying their names back; **artisans**, whose hands are worth too
much to risk in the sun; **soldier-slaves**, the guard companies of the caravans;
**laborers and farmers**, the numberless base of everything.

And understand the courts, such as they are: **there are no jails on Aerun.** A
jail, an Aerunite will tell you, is a warehouse that eats. Debt, theft, contraband,
unlicensed magic — every sentence resolves to the same two doors: the pits, for a
term or for life, or the arena, where a defendant with more courage than patience
may fight the term down. Judgment is quick, appeal is to the sand, and the House
recovers its costs either way.

**The exception is Tyr.** The Free City manumitted its pits within living
memory, in the upheaval that also left its champion's seat empty — the Tyrians
killed **Kalak**, their own undefeated champion, when he set himself above the
family, and the blood-letting freed the city's slaves before it was done. Vordon
remains a great House in every other particular: same Code, same registry, same
gate questions. But in Tyr the crane gangs are paid, the pits fight for purses
instead of sentences, and six rival kings wait with great patience for the
experiment to fail. It has not failed yet. Free labor, the Vordon factors point
out, cannot be seized against your debts — and prices in Tyr say what the other
six will not.

## Wizards: Defilers and the Veiled

Somewhere in every city, despite everything, there is magic.

The **defilers** are why you will be searched at the gates. Their art rips the
life from soil and stem — a defiler casting in a garden leaves a circle of ash —
and the Silt Sea stands as their monument. They practice in cellars and in the
wastes, and when one is caught the arena crowd is very large.

The **preservers** take only what the land can spare, and it is Aerun's
particular tragedy that no gate clerk can tell the difference. So the preservers
hide, banded into the quiet network the streets call the **Veiled Alliance** —
part conspiracy, part gardeners' order, working in secret toward a green Aerun
none of them will live to see. Officially every House condemns them. Actually,
several Houses find it useful that someone, somewhere, knows how to make things
grow. If you seek the Veiled, I cannot help you. If they seek you, be worth
their trouble.

## The Peoples of Aerun

Here is the second thing that will disorient you, after the missing temples:
**Aerun has no races.** It has the same peoples you know from your D&D — humans,
dwarves, elves, halflings, half-giants, thri-kreen, and the rest, men and women
in every trade and every rank alike — but nowhere on this continent does anyone
sort themselves by blood. There are no elf quarters, no dwarf-holds, no human
guilds. There are Houses. A Stel dwarf and a Stel elf are kin; a Stel dwarf and
a Vordon dwarf are competitors.

Give a continent a few centuries of that — every people working, living, and
marrying inside mixed households, with the Spice on the wind and in the water
doing whatever it is the Spice does to the boundaries between us — and blood
answers ambition. The old lines blur gladly here, in ways the sages of other
lands insist are impossible:

- **Muls** — human and dwarf: the tireless broad-backed folk who are the pride
  of every pit and crane gang.
- **Derrow** — elf and dwarf: rare elsewhere, common here; a dwarf's patience
  behind an elf's eyes, and the finest ledger-masters and navigators on the
  continent.
- **Half-giants** — bred first for the champions' stables, now a people in their
  own right, gentle until paid not to be.
- **Half-elves** — barely worth the name here; nobody counts.
- And the third-generation minglings the streets call **mutts** with no malice
  at all — a little of everything, sorted like everyone else by House and trade.
  On Aerun the question is never *what* are you. It is *whose* are you.

The **thri-kreen** deserve special note for the traveler who has met them only
as wasteland terrors: here they are citizens, packmates contracting as
caravan-guards and champions of real fame. A kreen's House loyalty, once given,
is the standard the rest of us are measured against.

**The blue eyes.** One marker does sort the peoples of Aerun, and it is not
blood but Spice. Heavy, sustained use stains eye-white and iris alike a deep
uniform blue — and the Spice is so ruinously dear that the stain is the surest
rank-sign on the continent. Blue eyes mean one of three things: great wealth,
a champion's provision, or the deep desert, where the free people come by it
honestly. A beggar with blue eyes is a story walking, and you should hear it.

## Villages

Between the cities, life gathers at water. **Client villages** farm, mine, or
herd under a House's glyph and protection, provisioned by the caravans they
supply. **Slave villages** do the same under harder terms, worked by pit-labor
serving out sentences under an overseer's Will. And every village, whatever its
charter, is the same crowd you find in the cities — every people mixed at the
same wells, muls and derrow and mutts in the same families. Travelers who ask
where the elf villages are get the smile again. There is no such place on Aerun.

## The Tribes

Beyond the walls and the road, the free peoples — and note the word: *free*, not
lawless. Outsiders expect the wastes to be sorted into elf tribes and dwarf
clans and kreen packs, as other deserts are. Aerun's tribes assemble around
**work, not blood**, and around one figure always: a **psionic master**, part
judge, part teacher, part quartermaster of the tribe's shared Will.

- **Herding tribes** follow the beasts along the scrub margins and sell to
  the waystations.
- **Raiding tribes** work the badlands and tax what the Code does not protect.
  Pay your caravan shares; ride with guarded trains.
- **Salvage tribes** mine the ruins of the old world and sell what they do not
  understand — often wisely.
- **Slave tribes** — escaped pit-labor banded together beyond the registry's
  reach. The Houses post standing bounties; the tribes post better guides.

And apart from all of these, the **desert people** — the free folk of the deep
sands beyond the Rim Wall, who owe no House, follow the great worms across the
silt basins, and harvest the Spice in their wake. They are the finest desert
craft in the world, blue-eyed to the last soul, and they enter the cities
seldom, trade briefly, answer the gate question with a look, and leave. Every
absurd story you have heard about them is false, and so is every sensible one.

## The Schools and the Masters

Last, the institution that stitches all of this together. Every city keeps its
**psionic schools** — public halls where any wild talent, freeborn or pit-born,
may be tested and trained, because a House that let talent rot in its pits
would be handing the next generation's champions to its rivals. The schools are
the one ladder that reaches all the way down. Masters sit where other lands
seat priests: at namings, at contract-swearings, at deathbeds, steadying the
mind of the House the way a keel steadies a ship. And the Spice stands behind
all of it — the rich buy focus, the champions are bound with it, the desert
people are born to it, and the schools teach you to make do on discipline,
which is cheaper and hurts more.

# Chapter Three: Aerun Geography

Every land I have walked is arranged sensibly: the water low, the mountains
high, the desert off to one side where you can decline to visit it. Aerun is
arranged like nothing else in the world, and you must fix its shape in your
mind before the rest of this chapter can help you.

**The dead heart of Aerun is above you.** The living coast — the cities, the
fields, the Ring Road — is the low country, a green-and-dun ribbon around the
continent's edge. Behind it, wherever you stand, a wall of ochre cliffs climbs
into the haze: the Rim Wall, the pedestal of the interior. And on top of that
pedestal, a thousand feet and more above the sea, lies the desert — and lying
upon the desert, filling the whole vast bowl of the highland, the Sea of Silt.
From the road you look *up* at the deadliest country on the continent. Where
the rim dips, the dust spills over the lip in slow gray cascades — the
silt-falls, smoking down the cliffs like waterfalls that have forgotten how to
hurry. They are beautiful, they are visible for miles, and they are the desert
reminding the coast where the border is.

## The Sea of Silt

Do not call it sand. Sand is honest; you can stand on sand. The silt is dust
ground finer than flour, pale as ash, dead flat to every horizon, and it flows
like water and drowns like water. A man walking onto it is gone in forty feet.
There is no swimming in it — it will not bear you up — and no bottom worth
reaching. It fills the whole interior of Aerun, hundreds of miles of gray, and
it is the reason the middle of our continent appears on charts as an absence.

There are three ways across: the lane, the shoals, and not at all.

**The lane** is the Caravan Guild's: the **Long Reach**, three hundred and
fifty miles of staked route running southwest from Raam's shelf to Balic's
crest — the fastest road on Aerun. Freight that crosses in five or six days by
skimmer convoy would spend three or four weeks going round by the Ring Road,
and the Guild's rates are priced accordingly, to the copper, with no haggling.
The route is marked end to end with mekillot ribs sunk upright in the dust and
capped with black obsidian to catch the sun; in clear air the next marker is
always in sight. Three squat towers stand on the firmest shoals along the way —
**First Mark**, a day out from Raam; **Midreach**, in the middle of the middle
of nowhere; and **Saltbone**, named for what the crews dug up sinking its
foundations, about which no one has ever wanted to know more. Beacon, cistern,
rope-yard, barracks: miss your waystation and you spend a night on the open
silt, which is the polite way convoy masters describe the worst thing that can
happen to a schedule.

Learn the lane law before you board; every hand aboard enforces it. Stay
between the markers. Pay the rate. Loaded vessel has the right of way. Answer a
beacon, always — even smugglers keep that one. And the fifth rule, which the
crews recite without smiling: **stop for nothing that hails you from the gray.**

**The silt ships** themselves are the one place Aerun has made a servant of the
wind that torments it everywhere else. Broad-hulled, shallow-drafted skimmers
and tall schooners run under sail across the dust, riding on it the way a flat
stone rides on water — kept riding, crucially, by the third member of every
crew that matters: the **navigator**. The Guild's navigators are the only souls
alive who can read the silt — where it will bear a hull, where it thins over
nothing, where the wake ahead of you is wind and where it is something else.
They stand hooded at the bow, always hooded, and they do not steer by their
eyes; they see the lane with the Will, the way you feel your own hand in the
dark. Silt blinds everyone equally, a navigator once told me; the difference is
what you have left when it does. If you ever contrived to lift one of those
hoods — and I advise against the attempt on several grounds — you would find
the deep-blue eyes of the heaviest Spice use on the continent. The Guild does
not confirm this. The Guild does not confirm anything.

**The shoals** are the desert people's: ridges of firmer ground beneath the
dust, shifting with the seasons, known to the Sandwalker tribes and to no one
else. They cross on foot where a convoy cannot follow, and they do not sell the
routes. Do not buy a shoal map in a Raam dockhouse. The man selling it has
never used it, which is why he is alive to sell it.

**And the worms.** Something large moves beneath the surface wherever the silt
runs deep, and the crews watch for the wake the way sailors watch for reefs.
On the shelves and margins the worms are cart-sized, dangerous the way any
large animal is dangerous. Out where the lane crosses the deep basins the
wakes are longer, and the crews stop pointing at them. The rule the whole
continent knows, though few have tested it: **the deeper the silt, the bigger
the worm.** The Sandwalkers do not fear them; they follow them — the worms lead
them across the gray, and where the great ones have passed, the tribes harvest
the Spice. What the connection is between the worm and the Spice, no coastal
scholar can tell you and no Sandwalker will.

One more thing, because you will hear it on every deck: the lane holds to the
eastern and southern reaches and **never bends northwest**. No markers run
there, no charts cover it, and the Guild's rule is absolute. Pilots call that
quarter *the blank* and change the subject. I have asked navigators, waystation
crews, and one very drunk convoy master, and I have gotten shrugs, silence, and
a request to talk about anything else. Whatever is in the northwest, the people
who know the silt best have decided you and I are better off ignorant, and I
have reached an age where I take professionals at their word.

## The Plateau Walls and the Portage Cities

Other continents have estuaries — soft country where river meets sea and
everything mingles. Aerun's waters do not mingle. The ocean stops at the coast,
the silt stops at the rim, and between them stands two thousand feet of
columnar ochre rock. Where those two seas come nearest to touching, we build a
city, and the city grows rich on the fact that they never quite do.

Only two crossings matter to trade. **Raam's shelf**, in the northeast, where
the wall steps down in benches and ramps and cargo can be worked from silt
quay to city warehouse to road. And **the Balic Saddle**, in the south, where
the whole Rim Wall stoops to a few hundred feet and the silt, finding the one
downhill on the continent, pours through the notch in a broad gray ramp that
stops dead against the rock Balic is built into. Stand in Balic's ocean harbor
and look up: the masts of silt schooners ride along the skyline above the
city, hulls moored on dust a quarter-mile over your head. The locals have
stopped noticing. You will not stop noticing.

Between the upper quays and the lower harbor, everything moves on muscle —
Balic's terraced crane-galleries, the **Stair of Sails**, hang off the outer
cliffs, and inside the rock the **Cochlea** winds down, a spiral stair broad
enough for freight gangs walking four abreast. This is the portage in its
purest form, and it is worth pausing on, because it is Aerun in miniature:
here is a place where a northern ship's cargo comes off the ocean, crosses
the continent's tolls, and goes up a cliff on the backs of ten thousand
laborers to catch the silt-wind for Raam — three portages deep, every one of
them taxed, and every tax flowing up through a lesser family's ledger to a
House. The equatorial winds made hemisphere sailing impossible; the Rim Wall
made the interior a fortress; and the seven cities sit on the seams, charging
the world for the inconvenience of geography. No army in history has managed
to hold a portage city. Every House in history has.

## Ruins, and the City of Plenty

The old world is up there, under the gray. The salvage tribes work the margins
where the silt runs shallow, and what they bring down — glass that bends,
metal that remembers its shape, engine-bones no smith can melt — sells in
every emporium on the road, usually to buyers wiser than the diggers.

And out of the deep silt comes the oldest story on Aerun: the **City of
Plenty**. Green towers above the dust. Fountains that never fail. The last
whole city of the old civilization, sealed and waiting, stuffed with the
wealth of a world that could make anything. Every generation the story gets a
new name, a new bearing, and a new crop of believers; the Sandwalkers, who
would know, say only that the deep silt keeps what it takes. One town has made
an industry of the dream: **Eldorado**, up on Tyr's spur of the rim, the last
place to buy rope before the high country and the first place to hear a lie
about what's up there. Its outfitters sell climbing irons, dust-veils, and
water at prices that assume you are not coming back to complain, and its one
long street is stocked with second-hand gear — sold, as they say up there, by
the people who returned and by the families of the people who didn't. I do
not say the City of Plenty is false. I say Eldorado's graveyard is full, its
merchants are rich, and both of those facts are downstream of the same story.

## The Tablelands

Between the coast's green edge and the foot of the Rim Wall lies the belt the
Ring Road actually threads: the Tablelands, the dry country, where Aerun keeps
all the desert it can fit below the rim. The road picks its way from well to
well through half a dozen terrains, and you should know them apart, because
they kill in different ways.

**Stony barrens** — bedrock scoured bare, loose plates of stone, no shade, no
forage, nothing to burn. Crossed quickly or not at all. **Sandy wastes** —
honest rolling dunes, yellow and orange, burying and unburying ruins on a
schedule of their own. **Rocky badlands** — mazes of wind-carved pillars and
slot canyons; the raiding tribes' country, where a caravan's outriders earn
their bread. The worst of it is the **Rust-Red Badlands**, iron-stained canyon
country where the rock bleeds orange and compasses of every kind — magnetic,
magical, psionic — are famously untrustworthy. **Scrub plains** — thorn,
saltbush, and tough gray grass, which passes for lush here; the herding tribes
work these margins. **Salt flats** — white, level, blinding, and hotter than
everywhere else, which I did not previously believe was possible.

Set into this country are the landmarks every drover knows: the **Dragon's
Bowl**, the great sunken basin with its haze of green at the bottom; **Lake
Pit**, the deepest open water anyone will show you, at the bottom of a hole
with sides; **Bitter Well**, an oasis whose name is a fair warning and whose
water is drunk anyway; and **Yaramuke and the Black Oasis** — a dead city and
the dark water beside it, both with reputations, neither on any House's
schedule of stops. The **Mekillot Mountains** squat over the southeastern
road, and that stretch is the one place the Ring Road's neutral-ground custom
is honored mostly in the telling; hire your guards before that leg, not
during. And east of Gulg spreads the **Crescent Forest** — the one true wood
below the rim, green, wet, close, and owned: Inika's foresters meet you at
the eaves, and the road, wisely, does not go in.

## The Ringing Mountains

The spine of the west coast, running unbroken from the headlands above Tyr
down past Osgaker and Hollowstorm to the volcano that smokes beside Urik.
Four to six thousand feet of rock, snow on the high crests — the only snow on
Aerun; children in Draj do not believe in it — and the whole reason the
continent is what it is. The westerlies come off the ocean carrying the rain
of half a world, strike this wall, climb, and surrender everything on the
seaward slopes. That rain is Tyr's green valley, Hollowstorm's fisheries, and
Urik's plantations. What crosses the crest arrives hot and empty and stays
that way for a thousand miles. One range; two continents' worth of weather;
and every drop of the difference taxed at a House gate.

The range pays in three metals: **iron** in the north above Tyr, **obsidian**
in the south above Urik, and **gold** at Walis in between, about which the
less said in print the better for you. The seaward faces are terraced and
farmed to the treeline; the landward faces are scree that runs when you stand
on it. The passes are few, guarded, and closed by weather more often than the
coast admits. And the name is earned: certain high canyons sing when the wind
pours through them, a long stone note you will hear before you see anything.
The miners say the mountains ring for the rain they keep. The Sandwalkers say
they ring for everything on the far side that the rain never reaches. Both,
I think.

## The Ring Road

Fourteen hundred miles of graded roadbed, wells, and waystops, circling the
continent and touching every city — the oldest continuously maintained thing
on Aerun, and the reason it is one place rather than seven. Neighbor cities
sit four to seven days apart: close enough to quarrel, far enough that
marching an army is ruinous, which tells you the road's builders understood
this continent perfectly.

The traffic is the spectacle. House caravans, courier strings, herds, walkers
beyond counting — and above them all the Caravan Guild's **mekillot
argosies**: rolling fortress land-trains the size of villages, drawn by beasts
the size of small hills. The Guild breeds and maintains the mekillot herds
itself, and every argosy is steered by a Guild **pilot** bonded mind-to-beast
— a driver who does not hold reins but *is*, for the length of the haul, some
calm tenth part of the animal. A good pilot can walk six thousand tons of
beast and cargo through a market crowd without bruising a melon. Pilots hood
themselves like their cousins on the silt, and for the same unspoken reason;
when the Guild trains your Will to fill something that large, the Spice is
part of the curriculum, and the eyes show it.

The caravans themselves are **shared** — this surprises every visitor. No
House owns the argosies; the Guild carries for all seven under charter, the
road's upkeep is split among the families whose trade depends on it
(grudgingly, and you can tell which family maintains which stretch by the
state of the wells), and the road itself is neutral ground by a custom older
than any current House. Feuds are not conducted on it. This holds well in the
west, indifferently in the north, and near the Mekillot Mountains it holds
the way a sieve holds water — but that the custom exists at all, on this
continent of beautifully organized greed, tells you what everyone here knows
in their bones: the road is the one possession no House can afford to win
from the others.

The full circuit runs Draj, Blackguard, Raam, Nibenay, Gulg's forest track,
Amber Valley, Balic, Urik, Hollowstorm, Osgaker, Tyr, and home to Draj —
roughly two months behind a caravan, a year if you stop to look at anything,
a lifetime if you are me. The two shortcuts are both silt: the Long Reach,
which you may buy passage on, and the Sandwalker shoals, which you may not.

# Chapter Four: An Atlas of Aerun, Briefly

A proper atlas of this continent would outweigh a water barrel, and your
gazetteer already holds the particulars of every town, oasis, and ruin worth a
bearing. What follows is the short tour — the seven cities as a traveler meets
them, gate by gate, with a drover's word on the places between. At every gate
the question is the same — *"What's your business?"* — but no two cities ask
it alike, and you can learn most of what matters about a House from the way
its clerks pronounce those three words.

## The Seven Cities

**TYR — House Vordon.** The Free City, in its green valley under the iron end
of the Ringing Mountains, and the gate question here is asked almost gently —
Tyr wants your business to go well. The cranes are worked by paid gangs, the
pits fight for purses, and the champion's seat has stood empty since the city
killed Kalak and emptied its own slave-pens in the same red week. Iron built
it; free labor is its wager against the world; and six patient rival kings
watch it the way creditors watch a debtor who has, so far, infuriatingly,
kept every payment. Behind the city the road climbs the spur to Eldorado and
the high passes.

**DRAJ — House Tsalaxa.** Grain and hemp out of the mud flats, and the
hardest bargainers on the road. Draj keeps the swagger of the old warrior
culture — the drums, the feathered regalia, the arena hymns — with no army
under it at all; Tsalaxa long ago learned to spend champions where others
spent legions, and their champion **Tectuktitlay** has kept the granaries'
prices honest for a generation. At the gate they ask your business like an
accusation. Answer anyway.

**RAAM — House M'ke.** The northeastern shelf-city, where the Long Reach
comes down off the silt, and the richest, loudest, most crowded, most
quarrelsome place on the continent. M'ke is a House at war with itself —
factions, cousins, rival ledgers — held together mainly by **Abalach-Re**,
whose retirement from the arena every faction dreads for the same reason.
Everything is for sale in Raam, including the answer to your gate question,
which the clerk will suggest you purchase pre-approved. Do.

**NIBENAY — House Shom.** Old money, old stone, old silence. Shom is the
House that is never seen negotiating: contracts arrive complete, prices are
not discussed, and the **Shadow King** has never fought a public bout —
opponents concede, which is either the most frightening fact on Aerun or the
best-run rumor in history, and Shom is content with either. The gate clerks
ask your business in a whisper and write down more than you said.

**GULG — House Inika.** Off the road, up the forest track, inside the eaves
of the Crescent Forest — the thorn city, grown as much as built. Feathers,
dyes, rare woods, and everything else on the continent that is green and
portable, all of it husbanded with a forester's patience. The **Oba's**
devotees will tell you she fights like the forest itself; Inika's factors
will tell you what that observation costs at current rates. The gate question
is asked at the treeline, and if you hear it, you were seen an hour ago.

**BALIC — House Wavir.** The Saddle city, with its two harbors stacked a
quarter-mile apart and everything between them moving on muscle — the Stair
of Sails outside the cliff, the Cochlea winding inside it. Balic is the
navigator trade, the salt and silver trade, and half the Guild's silt
tonnage; **Andropinis** has held the champion's sand so long that Wavir's
lesser families price bouts against him as acts of weather rather than war.
The gate clerks are the politest on the continent. They can afford to be;
you are standing in the toll booth of the southern hemisphere.

**URIK — House Stel.** The armory of Aerun, under its smoking mountain:
obsidian, kank-hide, drilled guard companies for hire, and the finest
fighting pits in the world. Stel's borders are the tightest of the seven and
its gate question comes with a search you will remember — but its arena is
the continent's cathedral, and on bout days the city is more open, and more
dangerous, than anywhere else you will ever stand. Above it all, undefeated
across a span no one honestly remembers the start of, sits **Hamanu**, the
champion the others are measured against, whose name closes arguments in six
cities and starts them only here.

## The Places Between

The road towns, west to east as the circuit runs, each with its page in the
gazetteer: **Hollowstorm** and **Osgaker** under the mountains' wet face,
fisheries and terrace-farms feeding half the west; **Blackguard**, the hard
stop on the northern leg between Draj and Raam; **Amber Valley**, where
caravan hiring is done openly at dawn and reputations are checked by men who
remember everything; **Altaruk**, **Salt View**, and **Ogo** along the
southern and eastern legs, each a walled well with opinions; **Walis**, high
in the Ringing Mountains, about which see above, and say I said nothing; and
**Eldorado** on its rim spur, selling rope and hope at the same counter.

Beyond the towns, the drover's landmarks: the Dragon's Bowl and its impossible
green floor; Lake Pit's deep cold water; Bitter Well, which is; Yaramuke and
the Black Oasis, which are avoided; the Rust-Red Badlands, where nothing that
points north can be trusted; the Mekillot Mountains, where you hire your
guards early; and over it all, always, the Rim Wall and its silt-falls,
smoking gray against the sky — the desert above, leaning on its fence,
looking down at the money.

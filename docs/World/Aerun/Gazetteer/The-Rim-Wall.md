# The Rim Wall

The ring of cliffs that holds up the interior — a continuous wall of columnar ochre rock separating the living coastal lands from the gray country above. Wherever you stand on the Ring Road, it is the horizon.

## At a glance

- **The climb** Around two thousand feet from the lowlands to the crest in most places, sheer at the top over long fans of scree. On the inner side, the silt comes almost to the lip: the wall is a rampart, not a canyon rim.
- **It is not uniform.** Highest and most brutal in the north and west, where the Ringing Mountains merge with it. Along the eastern sweep it steps down into benches and ramps. And at **[the Balic Saddle](The-Balic-Saddle.md)** it comes down to a few hundred feet — the one place a road can cross.
- **Silt-falls** Where the rim dips, dust spills over the edge in slow permanent cascades, smoking down the cliff face like gray waterfalls. Visible for miles; beautiful; also a sign the wind is up.

## Traveler's notes

- Only two ways over it matter commercially: **Raam's shelf** in the northeast and **Balic's saddle** in the south. Everything else is a climb, and climbers do not come back with cargo.
- The high passes — Eldorado's above Tyr among them — are used by prospectors and desert traders, not by trade.
- Things fall off the wall. Camping directly beneath a silt-fall or a scree fan is a beginner's mistake with an experienced person's consequences.
